# Structural study: McKay, Wood & Brantley, *The Dialectical Behavior Therapy Skills Workbook*, 2nd ed. (New Harbinger, 2019)

Paraphrased structural study for product design. No book text is reproduced; skill names and DBT
acronyms are named as terms of art, everything else is described in my own words. Source: plain-text
export, 9,308 lines. Chapter line offsets given in the brief were accurate.

Conventions used below for exercise SHAPE:

- **checklist** — a menu of pre-written items the reader ticks, always ending with blank "your own" lines
- **worksheet** — labelled fields to fill in (fields named in my words)
- **rating** — a numeric scale (what is rated, which scale)
- **script** — a guided practice with step-by-step instructions meant to be read first, remembered, or recorded in one's own voice and played back
- **plan** — a document the reader assembles from earlier picks, usually copied onto a card or into a phone note
- **log** — a table kept over days/weeks
- **reflection** — a one-off set of written questions about a past or future situation
- **reading** — concept exposition with worked vignettes, no writing asked of the reader
- **quiz** — a self-assessment with a scoring rule

---

## 1. The book's own framing

- **Audience.** Two named audiences: (a) people already in DBT (group or individual) who want a companion workbook, and (b) anyone struggling with what the book calls "overwhelming emotions", using it alone. The intro adds a hedge: if you are working alone and find the skills hard to implement, the authors strongly recommend finding a qualified DBT therapist. The preface notes DBT's original target was borderline personality disorder and that the skills are now used for anxiety, depression, shame, PTSD, substance-relapse prevention, anger and more.
- **Severity assumed.** High. The very first exercise is a checklist of self-destructive coping that includes cutting/burning/hair-pulling, suicide attempts, reckless driving, dangerous substance use, unsafe sex, binge/purge/restrict eating. Worked vignettes throughout involve suicidal ideation, an overdose attempt, cutting, arrest, and abusive relationships. Origins are framed as either inborn temperament or childhood trauma/neglect, but changeable.
- **"Dialectical" in its telling.** Holding two apparently contradictory things at once; the central pair is change and acceptance — change the behaviours that create suffering while accepting yourself as you are. Restated in ch. 2 (radical acceptance section) and ch. 7 (observing without judging).
- **How to use the book.** Four skill modules, each split into a "basic" and an "advanced" chapter; distress tolerance and mindfulness each get a third chapter. Basic chapters teach the concept, the components, and first steps; advanced chapters add the remaining components "level by level". The new 2nd-edition chapter 11 (exposure-based cognitive rehearsal) is for practising any skill while emotionally activated; chapter 12 integrates everything into a ~15-minute daily routine. Chapters 4, 5, 7 and 8 explicitly say to do their exercises in the printed order because each builds on the previous. The distress-tolerance chapters say to mark which skills help as you go, because later plans are assembled from those marks. The reading order is DT → mindfulness → emotion regulation → interpersonal → rehearsal → integration. No overall duration is stated; individual exercises carry their own cadences (two weeks here, a week there, "daily" often).
- **Behavioural contract.** The intro insists that reading changes nothing and asks the reader to write three current unhealthy reactions to emotion that they commit to replacing. Everything is framed as practice, not insight.
- **Companion materials.** Nearly every worksheet is flagged as downloadable from the publisher's site; three passages cross-reference numbered cards in a separate "DBT Skills Card Deck" (do tasks mindfully, see the big picture, observe body language); ch. 2 plugs the authors' book *The New Happiness* as a sequel on spirituality/values.
- **Safety/professional help in the framing.** Beyond the "get a DBT therapist if stuck" line, the book routes specific problems to professionals (eating problems, substance withdrawal, medical conditions before physiological techniques, sleep disorders, illness). There is no crisis plan, no crisis-line number, and no "stop using this book if…" criteria. See §4.

## 3. Cross-cutting structures

**Menu → plan → card.** The dominant device. Chapter 1's seven distraction checklists and five sense checklists exist so that the reader can assemble a Distraction Plan and a Relaxation Plan (home + away). Chapters 1–3 marks feed the New Coping Strategies worksheets, which feed the Emergency Coping Plan (two ordered four-step lists), which is later extended with physiological skills. Chapter 12 builds the daily routine from a five-slot menu. Every plan ends with the same instruction: copy it onto a card in your wallet or a phone note so it is available when thinking is impaired.

**Carry-with-you artefacts (explicitly told to keep on your person or in the phone).** Distraction plan; away-from-home soothing list (10 items plus props: candy, music, pictures, scent cards, a soft cloth, a photo of someone cared for, a written prayer/saying); five favourite coping thoughts; self-affirming statements (mirror/sticky/phone, checked daily); both emergency coping plans on one card; Coping Thoughts Worksheet and Big-Picture Evidence Log blanks to fill on the spot; judgment records folded in the pocket; a card of the legitimate rights on the bathroom mirror; recorded relaxation/meditation audio on the phone.

**Logs (kept over days).** Coping Thoughts Worksheet (situation → thought); HIIT Exercise Log (with mood before/after 0–10); Negative Judgments Record and Beginner's Mind Record (when/where/what); Weekly Mindfulness Activities Record (minutes per practice + what/where); Recognizing Your Emotions (daily for two weeks); Emotional Record (said aloud yes/no); Recognizing Your Self-Destructive Behaviors (two weeks); Big-Picture Evidence Log; Pleasurable Activities Log (daily); Emotion Log (one week, feeds exposure targets); Opposite-Action Planning Worksheet (with an outcomes column monitored over weeks); Weekly Regulator (Mon–Sun tick grid + three positive events per day); Conflict Log (≥ a week); Listening Blocks (three incidents); DBT Diary (weekly counts). The recurring log spine is: when/where → what happened → what I felt → what I did → what followed.

**Filled example before every blank.** Almost every worksheet and log is preceded by a fully filled example from a named vignette character, then the blank. The book's own explanation: the example shows the grain of detail expected.

**The retrospective re-run.** A repeated reflection template: describe a real past situation (what happened / felt / did / consequences), then imagine it again with the new skill inserted and describe the different outcome. Used for REST (ch. 1), radical acceptance (ch. 2), physiological skills past and future (ch. 3), big picture (ch. 7), behaviour analysis (ch. 8), communication post-mortem (ch. 10), and made experiential in ch. 11.

**0–10 ratings.** Valued Living (importance vs effort); FTB-Cope (feeling vs threat); eye-movement distress before/after; HIIT mood before/after; urgency and vulnerability for request intensity; fear probability as a percentage; cognitive rehearsal intensity with the 4–6 window and a 2–3 point drop criterion.

**Guided scripts share one chassis.** Quiet room, distractions off, tell others not to disturb you, sit comfortably, a few slow belly breaths ("balloon"), often count exhalations 1–4; instructions say "read first and remember, or record on your smartphone in a slow even voice and play back"; bracketed cues tell the recorder where to pause and for how long (typically one minute per block, thirty seconds in the compassion meditations, two to three minutes at the end of some). Timers start at 3–5 minutes and grow to 8–15. There is no bundled audio; the user is the narrator. A few passages also point at third-party phone apps (breathing pacer with adjustable inhale/exhale, white noise, nature sounds) and at the publisher's audiobooks.

**Practice ahead vs use in the moment — stated explicitly.** Cue-controlled relaxation, slow breathing, PMR, eye movements, safe place and the mindfulness sits are all "practise in calm so it works in distress"; REST, distraction, self-soothing, "where are you now?", FTB-Cope, cold techniques, emotion exposure, big picture, modulating intensity, saying no and the conflict skills are "in the moment". Chapter 11 exists precisely because the book admits calm practice does not transfer by itself.

**Reminder engineering.** Sticky notes with a single word around the home and workplace, a new ring or bracelet, a random bell, phone reminders, an entry in the calendar for tomorrow's request practice, a fixed daily appointment with yourself.

**Grading basic → advanced and explicit ordering.** Within mindfulness and emotion regulation the exercises are declared cumulative and must be done in order; mindfulness sits explicitly extend into daily activities only after seated practice; PMR basic for weeks before shorthand; the assertiveness hierarchy climbs from rank 1 to 10; slow breathing steps down the rate chart one row at a time.

**Deliberate repetition across modules.** Mindful breathing appears in ch. 2, 4, 5 (regimen), 8 (closes every emotion practice) and 12; thought defusion in ch. 4, 5, 7 and 12; coping thoughts in ch. 2 and 7; radical acceptance in ch. 1, 2, 5 and 11; pleasurable activities in ch. 1 and 7; cognitive rehearsal in ch. 2 and 11; the loving-kindness phrases in ch. 5, 6 and 9. The authors say this overlap is intentional to aid learning and recall.

**Self-assessment instruments.** Self-destructive coping checklist; "mindless" checklist; Valued Living Questionnaire; trigger-thoughts checklist; Identify Your Style (scored odd/even); the "Shoulds"; listening blocks; the aversive-strategies and passive-strategies reflections; the Communication Effectiveness Checklist as a diagnostic.

**Acronyms/mnemonics used.** REST; FTB-Cope; FLAME (doing tasks mindfully); ABC (problem solving); RAVEN (negotiation). Note: the book does *not* use Linehan's ACCEPTS, IMPROVE, TIPP, PLEASE, DEAR MAN, GIVE or FAST labels; its content overlaps them (distraction, self-soothing, cold water/exercise/paced breathing, physical vulnerability, assertiveness scripts) under the authors' own names.

## 4. Safety content, by technique

**General.** Intro: if using the book alone and struggling to implement, seek a qualified DBT therapist. No crisis-line number, no safety plan, no means-restriction advice, no explicit stop criteria. A crisis/suicide hotline appears only as one line among 100+ items in the pleasurable-activities checklist.

**Self-harm and suicide (addressed directly).**
- Ch. 1 names cutting, burning, scratching, picking, hair-pulling, suicide attempts, and reckless risk-taking in its opening checklist and cost table (costs listed include death, infection, scarring, hospitalisation).
- Ch. 1 offers a harm-reduction substitute list explicitly framed as "less harmful until you can stop", including sensations that are themselves mildly painful (ice, rubber-band snap, fingernails without breaking skin). Presented without clinical oversight.
- Ch. 3's cold-pressor technique is explicitly positioned as reproducing the relief self-injury gives, citing BPD research, and is capped at 4 minutes and "never severe pain".
- Ch. 7 explains the endorphin reward of cutting and asks the reader to write their own behaviours, rewards and costs; a worksheet vignette records suicidal urges and cutting as ordinary entries.
- Ch. 8's exposure vignette has the character observe suicidal/vehicle-crash imagery as thoughts to be released; no escalation path is given.
- Ch. 9 lists "I'll hurt myself if you don't…" as a manipulation tactic (threatening), naming suicide threats as an aversive strategy.
- Ch. 2's radical-acceptance vignette involves an overdose attempt and arrest.

**Radical acceptance boundary.** Stated three times (ch. 1, 2, 5): acceptance does not mean tolerating abuse or violence; if you need to leave an abusive relationship, leave. Ch. 6: sending kindness to a difficult person is not permission for them to hurt you.

**Medical cautions (precise).**
- Ch. 3 chapter-level: balance, blood pressure, heart-rate conditions, heart disease, breathing problems, hypertension, pregnancy, or medication for any of these → consult a clinician first, because these techniques can drop heart rate and blood pressure quickly.
- Eye movements: stop if eye strain or pain, or slow down.
- Diving response: pregnancy, heart or blood-pressure conditions → consult first; never put a fully frozen pack directly on skin; stop immediately on pain; do not do anything else that blocks breathing (fainting, passing out, death named); prefer compress to full immersion; not while driving.
- Cold pressor: 0–10 °C, 2–4 min, timer capped at 4 min, mild-to-moderate discomfort only, stop on severe pain.
- HIIT: check with a medical provider first, especially with heart problems, stroke, diabetes, breathing trouble, chronic pain or joint problems; consult a fitness expert for unfamiliar equipment; warm up; no more than 2–3 sessions per week; never more than 20–30 min; don't escalate volume quickly.
- Slow breathing: dizziness, light-headedness, faintness or tingling in lips/fingers = hyperventilation → stop and breathe normally.
- Mindful breathing (ch. 2, ch. 4): light-headedness can occur → stop or return to normal rate.
- PMR: caution with back, neck, joint or shoulder pain; consult if pregnant or prone to fainting; never tense to the point of pain; avoid over-tensing feet/toes (cramping); release quickly.
- Ch. 7: consult a professional before strenuous exercise, on abnormal pain, on signs of a sleep disorder, on eating problems, on substance tolerance, and before stopping alcohol or drugs (withdrawal can be dangerous); follow prescriptions; report prescription side effects.
- Ch. 1: if eating is a problem (bingeing, purging, restriction) talk to a counsellor and use the other senses; massage options for abuse survivors who don't want to undress; don't listen to relaxation recordings while driving or operating equipment.

**Emotional-load cautions.**
- Ch. 4 single-object: nothing that could hurt you and nothing emotionally loaded.
- Ch. 4 Describe Your Emotion: wait if the current emotion is overwhelmingly sad or self-destructive.
- Ch. 3 eye movements: start with a *mildly* disturbing memory.
- Ch. 11: choose events that evoke a *moderate* reaction; cut the scene at 4–6 of 10; shift attention to the skill, not the scene. There is no explicit trauma contraindication and no advice against using traumatic memories — the only guard is the moderate-intensity ceiling and the instruction to shut the scene off.
- Ch. 6: loving-kindness may release grief or anger; framed as normal.
- Ch. 8 emotion exposure: start with periods as short as five minutes; always finish with mindful breathing.
- Ch. 9: angry dissociative states are linked to violence; watch for red flags and breathe.

**Toxic relationships.** Best solution is to get away; if unavoidable, calm first and pre-script.

## 5. Chapter 12's assembly

The book's integration is a fixed ~15-minute daily routine plus a weekly diary, not a "which skill when" decision aid (the closest thing to a decision aid is FTB-Cope in ch. 2, which routes between "act" skills and "cope" skills, and the Communication Effectiveness Checklist in ch. 10 for interpersonal post-mortems).

Daily Practices for Emotional Health — five slots, each chosen from a short menu, done at the same time every day as an appointment with yourself:

1. Mindfulness, 3–5 min: mindful breathing *or* wise-mind meditation.
2. Deep relaxation, 3 min: cue-controlled relaxation *or* band of light *or* safe-place visualization.
3. Self-observation, 3 min: thought defusion *or* being mindful of emotions without judgment.
4. Affirmation: one self-affirming statement (from ch. 2 or your own), repeated five times with slow breaths; same or different each day.
5. Committed action, 3 min: plan one concrete thing for today or tomorrow — a committed action from the values work, or a way to connect to your higher power (prayer, an act of kindness, giving).

The rationale maps slots to modules: mindfulness underpins everything; relaxation is distress tolerance; self-observation and affirmation are emotion regulation; committed action serves emotion regulation and interpersonal effectiveness. A one-page "My Daily Practices" form records the five picks and the time of day. Perseverance is framed as a commitment remade each day rather than made once. The DBT Diary is a weekly sheet counting how many times per day each key skill was used, with a specifics column for some rows; the sheet is download-only. Chapter 12 also reminds the reader to keep using exposure-based cognitive rehearsal.

There is no weekly schedule template beyond the diary and the earlier per-module weekly records (Weekly Mindfulness Activities Record, Weekly Regulator), and no guidance on how long to keep the routine going.

## 6. Teachable without a therapist vs. assumes one

**Fully self-contained (a workbook-only user can run it).** All scripts (the reader narrates their own recordings); all checklists, plans and logs; the physiological techniques (given medical clearance); the daily routine; cognitive rehearsal. The book says nothing that requires a group to be present.

**Places that assume a therapist/group or a second person.**
- Intro: audience (a) is people in DBT using this as their group workbook; the "get a therapist if stuck" fallback.
- Ch. 1 vignette: a character practises skills "with her therapist".
- Ch. 11 opening: "at home or in your DBT group".
- Ch. 10: the simple-request practice menu includes asking a teacher or therapist; assertiveness scripts are to be rehearsed alone "or with someone you trust".
- Ch. 5 hindrances: doubt is to be taken to mindfulness teachers and fellow practitioners.
- Ch. 8 problem-solving vignette relies on a spouse as a check before criticising anyone.

**Where a solo user is likely to get stuck.**
- **No answer keys** except the ten "I" statement rewrites. The reader judges for themselves whether a coping thought, opposite action, evidence entry or script is any good.
- **Big picture / evidence against**: the book admits this question is new and hard; a filtered reader may write "none". No prompts beyond "give it a few minutes".
- **Wise-mind decisions** rest on introspection plus checking outcomes over time; the book itself says the gut signal is hard to tell from emotion mind at first.
- **Recognizing Your Emotions step 2 ("why did it happen")** records the reader's attribution as-is; the worked example records a hostile attribution (husband drinks to hurt me) without correction, and the corrective (big picture) arrives many pages later.
- **Primary vs secondary emotions** and **behaviour analysis chains** require a level of self-observation the book acknowledges takes weeks of logging.
- **Emotion exposure with self-harm or suicidal urges**: the procedure says to notice urges and not act, and to end with breathing; there is no branch for "the urge is dangerous".
- **Assertiveness hierarchy, negotiation, saying no** need real counterparts and real stakes; the workbook can only stage them.
- **FTB-Cope threat ratings** are self-assessed by the same mind whose emotional reasoning is in question.
- **Physiological skills** hand the safety gate to "your medical professional", which an app cannot verify.
- **The daily routine's committed action slot** presumes the values work (VLQ, committed action) was completed.
- **Harm-reduction substitutes for self-injury** are offered with no clinician in the loop; some substitutes are themselves mildly painful stimulation.

## 8. Appendix A — Form and log schemas (one field per line, paraphrased)

Field names are my descriptions of what each slot asks for, not the book's labels. "Example row" means the book prints a filled specimen above the blank form.

### A.1 Cost of Self-Destructive Coping (ch. 1)
- row per strategy (12 pre-filled + 2 blank)
- pre-filled likely costs per strategy
- "other costs" free text per row
- example row: yes (the costs are the example)

### A.2 Take a REST (ch. 1)
- what happened
- how I felt
- what I did
- any self-destructive behaviour (yes/no + which)
- how I could have relaxed
- what an evaluation would have found
- what intention I would have set
- what action I would have taken and what would have followed
- overall advantage of having used REST

### A.3 My Distraction Plan (ch. 1)
- fixed first line: use REST
- free lines: chosen distraction skills
- export instruction: copy to sticky note / wallet / phone note

### A.4 Relaxation Plan (ch. 1)
- list A: soothing skills usable at home
- list B: soothing skills usable away from home (portable only)
- export: copy list B (10 items) to a card or phone; gather props

### A.5 Safe place (ch. 2)
- my safe place is …
- it makes me feel …
- then a guided script (record-yourself optional)

### A.6 Cue-controlled relaxation (ch. 2)
- my cue word is …
- per-session note: how long it took to feel relaxed (twice daily)

### A.7 Valued Living Questionnaire (ch. 2)
- 10 domains (family, romantic, parenting, friends/social, work, education, recreation, spirituality, community, self-care)
- per domain: ideal importance 0–10 (circle)
- per domain: actual effort 0–10 (square)
- derived: gap; work first on high-importance/low-effort domains

### A.8 Committed Action (ch. 2)
- 3 blocks, each: valued domain; intention; committed actions with start dates
- make many copies; never "complete"

### A.9 Higher power reflection (ch. 2)
- 9 open-text answers (see 2.6)

### A.10 Coping Thoughts Worksheet (ch. 2)
- 10 rows × (distressing situation; new coping thought)
- example row: yes (10 filled)
- carry a blank and fill it as soon as it happens

### A.11 Radical Acceptance questions (ch. 2)
- 11 open-text answers (see 2.13)
- example: fully worked vignette precedes

### A.12 FTB-Cope (ch. 2)
- emotion intensity 0–10
- threat items, two per emotion family (anger / anxiety-fear / guilt-shame / sadness), each 0–10
- take the maximum threat score
- rule: close → act (values, wise mind, problem solving); feeling ≫ threat → cope (menu of 10 skills)

### A.13 New Coping Strategies (ch. 2) — two sheets: with others / alone
- 4 rows × (distressing situation; old coping strategies; unhealthy consequences; new coping strategies; healthier likely consequences)
- example row: yes, one per sheet
- rule: be specific — name the exact thought, the exact time-out activity

### A.14 Emergency Coping Plan (ch. 2) — two lists: with others / alone
- first I'll …
- next I'll …
- then I'll …
- finally I'll …
- ordered by expected effectiveness; fall through on failure
- export: both plans on a single card or in the phone
- later addition: physiological skills from ch. 3

### A.15 Eye-movement drill (ch. 3)
- memory chosen (mildly disturbing)
- distress 0–10 before
- 30 s of movement
- distress 0–10 after
- repeat 4–5 times

### A.16 HIIT Exercise Log (ch. 3)
- date
- exercise performed
- length of high-intensity interval × number of intervals
- maximum intensity (heart rate % or talk-test level)
- length of moderate interval
- mood before, named + 0–10
- mood after, named + 0–10
- example row: yes

### A.17 Slow-breathing baseline (ch. 3)
- breaths per minute at rest (1-min count)
- chart lookup: seconds per cycle
- target: step down one row every several days/weeks toward 6/min
- inhale seconds / exhale seconds (exhale longer)

### A.18 Physiological skills reflections (ch. 3)
- past: situation; feeling; reaction; end result; which skill could have helped; how it would have felt; how I would have reacted; how it might have ended
- future: likely situation; what I think will happen; how I might react; most effective skill; how it will help; how it might go differently; how I'll remind myself

### A.19 Describe Your Emotion (ch. 4)
- emotion name (from the ~88-word list)
- drawing area
- related action
- related sound
- intensity (metaphor allowed)
- quality (metaphor allowed)
- related thoughts (must be thoughts, not more emotion words)

### A.20 Three minutes of thoughts (ch. 4)
- one or two words per thought for 3 min
- count; × 20 = per hour estimate

### A.21 Negative Judgments Record / Beginner's Mind Record (ch. 5)
- when
- where
- what the judgment was (negative only / both positive and negative)
- example rows: ~10 each
- carried folded in a pocket; ≥1 week; retained for judgment defusion

### A.22 Weekly Mindfulness Activities Record (ch. 5)
- week of
- per day Mon–Sun: mindful breathing minutes; wise-mind minutes; self-compassion minutes; task done mindfully (what, where); other mindful exercise ×2

### A.23 Recognizing Your Emotions (ch. 7)
- when it happened
- what happened
- why I think it happened (causes / meaning)
- primary emotions
- secondary emotions
- physical sensations
- what I wanted to do (urges)
- what I did and said
- how it affected me later (short and long term)
- example: yes; cadence one per day for ≥2 weeks

### A.24 Emotional Record (ch. 7)
- when and where
- how I felt ("right now I feel …")
- said it out loud? yes/no
- what I did after recognising it
- example rows: 8

### A.25 Rewards/costs fill-ins (ch. 7) — self-injury, manipulation, eating, substances
- the behaviours I engage in
- the temporary rewards
- the long-term costs and dangers
- (substances adds) how it affects how I feel; two ways to improve

### A.26 Physical-vulnerability fill-ins (ch. 7) — food, exercise, sleep, illness/pain, tension
- how this affects how I feel
- two ways I can improve / treat it

### A.27 Recognizing Your Self-Destructive Behaviors (ch. 7)
- same as A.23 through "what I did and said"
- last field: what the emotional reward was, and why it was only temporary
- example: yes; cadence two weeks

### A.28 Big-Picture Evidence Log (ch. 7)
- what happened
- what I thought and felt (specific)
- evidence supporting the thought/feeling
- evidence contradicting it
- a more accurate and fair way to think and feel
- what I can do to cope in a healthy way
- example: yes; carried; used in the moment

### A.29 Pleasurable Activities Log (ch. 7)
- when
- what I did
- how I felt
- what I thought
- example: a full week; daily

### A.30 Emotion Log (ch. 8)
- date
- event (internal or external trigger)
- emotion
- coping or blocking response
- example: 6 rows over 5 days; one week; reviewed for chronic emotions and harmful blocking

### A.31 Opposite-Action Planning (ch. 8)
- emotion
- emotion-driven behaviour (list)
- opposite action (concrete, incl. posture/voice)
- time period I'll hold it
- outcomes (filled in later)
- example: 2 rows

### A.32 Behavior Analysis (ch. 8)
- problematic emotion
- precipitating: external event; thoughts; prior emotion; behaviour
- secondary events a, b, c: each thoughts / emotion / behaviour
- selection rule: a link I control that would reduce the emotion

### A.33 ABC Problem Solving (ch. 8)
- alternatives brainstorm (per chosen link)
- best one or two
- commitment: time, place, exact new thought/behaviour written out

### A.34 Weekly Regulator (ch. 8)
- Mon–Sun tick grid, section 1 physical: illness/pain steps; balanced eating; no drugs/alcohol; enough sleep; exercised; relaxation/mindfulness for stress
- section 2 cognitive: observed trigger thoughts; used coping thoughts; noticed a positive event
- positive events: 3 lines per day
- section 3 watching/accepting: watched the emotion; didn't act on it; didn't judge it
- section 4 coping: opposite action; behaviour analysis; problem solving
- filled at the end of each week

### A.35 Identify Your Style (ch. 9)
- 12 tick statements; odd = passive, even = aggressive

### A.36 I want–they want (ch. 9)
- one relationship
- columns: I want; outcome; they want; outcome

### A.37 The Shoulds (ch. 9)
- 10 tick statements; more ticks = stronger self-denial

### A.38 Interpersonal values (ch. 9)
- behaviours that diminish self-respect (incl. omissions)
- my rules for how people should be treated
- which values I most often disregard
- how relationships are affected when I violate them

### A.39 Aversive / passive reflections (ch. 9)
- which strategies I recognise in myself
- impact on relationships when I used them
- do I want to change this, and why

### A.40 Conflict Log (ch. 9)
- date
- my need
- my behaviour
- aversive or passive strategy used
- consequences
- review after ≥1 week: triggering needs; most-used strategies; did they work; emotional consequences

### A.41 Red flags (ch. 9)
- my own warning signs of losing control (free list)

### A.42 Risk Assessment / Risk Planning (ch. 9)
- my fear
- evidence it will happen
- evidence it won't
- estimated % chance
- coping plan if it happens (resources, people, skills)

### A.43 Knowing What You Want (ch. 10)
- feeling in words (from the good/bad decision tree)
- what I want changed: more of / less of / stop / start
- when / where / how often
- one or more clear sentences

### A.44 Modulating intensity (ch. 10)
- urgency of my need 1–10
- vulnerability of the other person / relationship 1–10 (high = robust)
- sum → how forceful to be

### A.45 Assertiveness scripts (ch. 10)
- 3 problems × (the problem; what I want changed)
- 3 scripts × (I think; I feel; I want; how I'll take care of myself)

### A.46 Listening Blocks (ch. 10)
- 3 rows × (situation where communication broke down; which of the 10 blocks)

### A.47 Assertive Situation Hierarchy (ch. 10)
- 10 ranked situations (1 easiest … 10 hardest)
- per situation as reached: script; rehearsal; time and place; committed date; evaluation

### A.48 Negotiation compromises (ch. 10)
- 3 conflicts × 2 compromise types with implementation detail

### A.49 Communication Effectiveness Checklist (ch. 10)
- tick lists across 10 headings (see 10.11)
- then: chosen few items; written plan of what I'll do differently and where

### A.50 Cognitive rehearsal session (ch. 11)
- skill chosen (from 18)
- scene (recent or future)
- intensity 0–10 on entering the scene; cut off at 4–6
- skill practised until drop of 2–3 points
- repeat count; skills dropped as ineffective

### A.51 My Daily Practices (ch. 12)
- mindfulness pick
- deep relaxation pick
- self-observation pick
- affirmation
- committed action plan
- time of day
